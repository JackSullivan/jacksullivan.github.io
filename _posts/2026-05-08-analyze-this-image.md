## But if you close your eyes

This is what Gemini sees when it closes its eyes. A samoyed surrounded by 'rigid geometry' that looks like it was generated by AI. Well, that last part is unsurprising, but what about the rest? 

First, why does it see _anything_? Gemini, like all LLMs at the moment, is based on the Transformer architecture, which I won't cover here because others have done it both quite well, and to death{% sidenote 'transformer' "If you want an entry, you could do much worse than Jay Alammar\'s [The Illustrated Transformer](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/)." %}. 
I have a bunch of opinions about the transformer architecture, so I'll have to cut myself off here if I ever want to actually explain what's happening. 
<!-- Watch [[this space]] for more. -->
For our purposes, we can think of the transformer as a magic box that transforms inputs into output (why not, everyone else already does). 

A multimodal transformer like Gemini is really more of a `text++` encoder. 
The transformer typically directly receives text-based tokens and then has some way of integrating other modalities. 
In the case of Gemini, the typical Google way of doing this involves training a separate image encoding model and then interposing additional (cross-attention) layers into the text model that carries the information from the image. 
These layers are always _present_ in the model even when an image isn't in the input. 
The nature of the cross-attention mechanism is such that the text tokens interact with the image 'tokens' at the behest of the text tokens{% sidenote 'kqv' "Technically the text tokens are the 'query' for the attention mechanism, while the image tokens provide the 'key' and 'value'."%}. 
This means that in the typical case of a text-only input, the signal should pass through these layers with minimal change, since the text doesn't pertain to a specific image. 

When the input text _is_ about an image though, this cross-attention mechanism pulls signal out of the pre-trained image embedding model, whether or not an image is present. 
The 'image' that Gemini is describing is the result of running that image embedding model on an empty input. 
This is what Gemini _expects_ to see, when it doesn't see anything. 
So, why does it see what it does? Let's start with the dog.

## The dog days aren't over

{% maincolumn 'assets/images/monalisa_deepdream.jpg' "Remember DeepDream? Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeepDream#/media/File:%22Mona_Lisa%22_with_DeepDream_effect_using_VGG16_network_trained_on_ImageNet.jpg)" %}

Back in 2016, I was trying to convince people that this machine learning thing was practically useful and AlphaGo and DeepDream were the most salient things that the general public had in mind when you talked about 'AI'{% sidenote 'tempora' 'O tempora! O mores!'%}. DeepDream's practical use was in transforming images into slightly horrifying mishmashes of psychedelic dog faces. 

DeepDream worked by essentially taking an image model that was trained on some classification task (ie. does this picture contain a bus or a tree) and running it 'backwards'. For any given true image, there is some difference between the pixels of the image and what the model 'expects' to see{% sidenote 'minloss' 'technically, the image that would minimize its loss' %}. Training a network takes its weights and adjusts them so that they are closer to true images. DeepDream takes an _image_ and adjusts _it_ so that it is closer to the network's expectations{% sidenote 'ddfancy' "There is a lot more fanciness involved so that the results come out looking coherent-ish that we don't need to cover"%}.

These models are trained to distinguish between different visual classes (bus or tree, etc.), and _what_ visual classes they distinguish between determine in part the features of an image are salient to them. 
Why do people see Jesus in a piece of toast? The same reason that car grills look vaguely like faces - because humans have a lot of visual processing power devoted to recognizing and differentiating faces. 
If an alien's brain (or a dog's for that matter) were wired up to a human visual field, it would probably perceive something like DeepDream images, but with human faces everywhere. 

Ok cool, but why dogs? Well, just like every other area of human endeavor, in ML the present is built on the past, and when you're busy building up you don't spend too much time looking down. 
For most of the past 30 years, ML has progressed from dataset to dataset, and in the modern era of model scaling, the field tends to take a yes-and approach to what should be used for training data. 
So, just as LLMs are trained by slurping up, soviet trawler-like, everything that humanity has written over the past few thousand years, image models are trained on all the captioned images they can find{% sidenote 'lioncsam' 'hopefully [without the CSAM](https://laion.ai/notes/laion-maintenance/)'%}. 

Way back in 2009, [ImageNet](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5206848) was one of the first large-scale{% sidenote 'oftime' 'by the standards of the time'%} image classification datasets. 
In 2011, as part of a fine-grained image detection task, a subset of this data containing dogs annotated with their breeds was produced, called the [Stanford Dogs Dataset](https://people.csail.mit.edu/khosla/papers/fgvc2011.pdf). 
Typically, models are trained with many of these datasets folded together, so you might think that 20k pictures of dogs should be washed away in the billions of other images that the model is trained on, but it doesn't work that way. 

When the model is being trained, it receives signal based on when it classifies things incorrectly.
Typically, this is done in an unweighted way. 
If the categories are `bus` and `tree`, the model doesn't change more{% sidenote 'aggblah' "in aggregate, it's more complicated than this, blah, blah"%} due to classifying a bus as a tree with a given confidence than vice versa. 
The Stanford Dogs dataset contains _120 different dog breeds_. 
That means that the model is equally incentivized to accurately distinguish between a Bull Mastiff and a Boxer as it is to distinguish between a bus and a tree.

{% maincolumn 'assets/images/twodogs.jpg' "Go on, you tell me which one's which. And I consider myself a dog person. Source: [Stanford Dogs Dataset](http://vision.stanford.edu/aditya86/ImageNetDogs/)" %}

This means that, even though dog images are small in the space of the _data_ they are large in the space of the _labels_, which pushes the model to allocate weight to recognizing dogs out of proportion to their prevalence. Even in the high-flying days of 2026, these 120 dog breeds are nestled away in the pre-training set of the image encoder, leaving an afterimage of a samoyed in Gemini.

## The rest of the owl

The rest of the hallucinated image description is a bit harder to parse, but there are still some lessons there. At this point though, it gets harder to separate clearly between what parts of the description come from the image encoder and what parts come from the transformer's interpretation of it. My take is that the primary features coming from the image encoder are the dog face{% sidenote 'multdog' "probably multiple, which leads to talking about the 'reflection in the floor'"%}, the 'rigid geometry', and the monochromaticity. The remainder 'futuristic lobby', 'atmosphere', and 'lighting' come from the transformer trying to describe what is essentially an incoherent image.

The monochromaticity isn't too surprising or interesting. It's the result of the fact that the normalized image preprocessing will tend to balance out different prevalences of color channels across the dataset. This means that, by the time the image gets to the encoder, there aren't imbalances in particular color channels that the _model_ has to learn. 

The rigid geometry is more interesting. The classic architecture for deep learning is the Convolutional Neural Net (CNN) which again I won't explain in depth{% sidenote 'cnn' 'See [here](https://stanford.edu/~shervine/teaching/cs-230/cheatsheet-convolutional-neural-networks/), or literally anywhere else, for more detail'%}.
The CNN was the first real breakout hit for the deep learning revolution, starting around [2012 or so](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf). The modern form of the architecture itself goes way back to the late 80's, and is a beautiful example of why it can be useful to fiddle away on ideas that everyone knows don't work. 


A CNN is made up of a sequence of convolution layers, which scan across patches of a 2D image and pooling layers which integrate those scans. The architecture is loosely inspired by V1-V4 of the mammalian visual cortex. 
One of the really cool things about them is that the convolution layers work pretty well [before you train them](https://robotics.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml11-RandomWeights.pdf){% sidenote 'dlvis' "Incidentally, this is probably part of why vision tasks were where deep learning first had major success in the early 2010s. At that time getting a training signal to propagate all the way to the 'bottom' of a network was a challenge. The convolutions in the early layers not needing training to be serviceable probably helped make these models work"%}. 

So what do these (trained or not) convolution layers tend to do? In early layers as in evolved visual cortices, they tend to detect edges, curves, and lines at different angles. When there is no image, some of these layers (due to bias weights) will be activated at different points in the image, leading to a locally coherent but globally incoherent pattern of edges, curves, and lines; a 'rigid geometry'. 

If you grew up in a certain pre-internet era and remember closing and covering your eyes and waiting for psychedelic, kaleidoscopic images to appear as a form of light entertainment, that is the same phenomenon happening inside your visual system. If you haven't done so in a while, give yourself a little treat and do that now. Then imagine a dog in the middle and see like Gemini.
